> A few quick observations from VibeHacks #5. Just my personal take, so go easy on me. I didn't have time to unpack everything; happy to talk more. Also, this post represents my own perspective, not VibeFriends as an organization.

Thanks to VibeFriends for the invitation. Last Saturday I went to VibeFriends' VibeHacks #5. For the first time, I was sitting in the audience looking at projects as a judge rather than as a participant.

![The theme: Vibe Coding for Leaving Work on Time](/images/posts/vibehacks-judge-notes-poster.jpg)

I got there early and walked around. Looked at projects, people, and what was on their screens. Back home, I pulled together a few notes from the day, mostly from the perspective of someone seeing your project for the first time: what gets my attention, and what makes my mind wander. Hopefully useful if you're getting on stage at the next one.

## 1. The screening starts before the pitches

Judges don't only start paying attention when you get on stage. Before the formal pitches, I'd already gone through the projects at the venue. These things directly shaped which ones I was looking forward to:

**The name and title need to tell people what you're doing.** Honestly, with some projects I had no idea what they were after reading the name. A name can be clever, but a subtitle that explains what you're doing, or what you're trying to say, helps.

**The cover image is the first thing people see.** The visuals decide whether I'll click through to the introduction first. (Of course I looked at all of them. I mean that a clear cover image made me open that project sooner.) This takes very little effort, but makes a big difference.

**Any sign of building in public.** I was noticeably more curious about projects that had an introduction on Xiaohongshu. Most hadn't had time to post anything. The ones that had, frankly, felt rushed and a little too obviously AI-written. There's a lot of room to do better here. Spend twenty minutes writing one thoughtful post and you'll already stand out from most of the room.

## 2. On stage: a good demo tells a complete story

![The pitches: three minutes per project](/images/posts/vibehacks-judge-notes-demo.jpg)

Three minutes is short, but it's enough to make one thing clear.

**A good demo tells a complete story, firmly from the user's point of view.** The same thing we all learn in our first product lesson: who is the user, what situation are they in, what problem do they have, and what will they use this product to do?

I saw the opposite, too. There was a video-editing project I'd honestly been looking forward to before they started. But they rushed through a minute of talking at us, and by the end I still didn't know what problem it solved. They covered the features. They didn't cover the problem.

**Another observation: there weren't many women competing, but several of them presented really well. (Thinking about what they had in common: expressive delivery and a strong sense of the user's perspective.)** One particularly stuck with me. Something went wrong with the screen sharing during her demo. With only three minutes in total, she handled it brilliantly. She didn't lose a second, just went straight into the user story and a few use cases. While she talked, a teammate stepped in and fixed the screen sharing without missing a beat. That's the kind of coordination a hackathon needs.

And this is something you can prepare for: **if the screen sharing fails, how will you use your three minutes?** If you've thought about it beforehand, you won't panic on the day.

## 3. What counts as fitting the theme?

![A packed audience for the pitches](/images/posts/vibehacks-judge-notes-audience.jpg)

The theme was "AI helps people leave work on time." If I were building something myself, I'd probably approach it from one of two angles:

**One is something genuinely AI-native.** For example, where do we get stuck when using all these agents? There's opportunity in those sticking points. I didn't see a project that immediately grabbed me from this angle. (I was hoping for something that wasn't reinventing the wheel or just wrapping it in a pretty UI. Of course, that's asking a lot of a project built in 24 hours. To be fair, I'd probably struggle to find a good angle myself.)

**The other is to start with people.** But there's a trap here: over the past few months, I've seen so many projects for office workers, people in tech, and AI practitioners.

One that really got me was a project called "On-Time Handover" (准点交班). It used AI to record and carry over patients' medical records, conditions, and things to watch for when healthcare staff handed over a shift. Listening to the demo and the examples, I could clearly tell that the person building it must have a healthcare background. (I asked afterwards, and they did.) There was another project I wish we'd seen more of: a water resources engineer tackling problems in their own field. Issues with the demo meant they couldn't fully show it.

So here's what I want to say: **AI that draws on different disciplines and professional backgrounds to solve real problems in real settings, who wouldn't like that?** We've already seen so much AI for office workers. That industry problem only you understand, the one other people don't even know exists, is your biggest advantage.

## 4. A little behind the scenes: what everyone was using

Walking around and looking at screens, I found the mix of tools interesting:

- **Codex was the clear favorite** (Tibo and Sam are winning big)
- **I only saw one or two teams using Claude Code** (my guess is that widespread account bans are the main reason)
- About two teams on workbuddy, with a handful using zcode and trae here and there
- I even saw a team using the tabbit browser, haha

If I'd done the same count six months ago, the results would have been completely different.
